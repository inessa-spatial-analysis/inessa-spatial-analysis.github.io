Where each party’s voters live

The charts and the winner map use small areas, about a city block or two. Each one takes the result of the nearest polling station.

These are places, not individual voters. “Where Likud is strong, the place has more people born in Africa” means those places have more residents born in Africa than the country as a whole.

Who lives where each party is strong

Pick a party. The sentence is the short portrait from the census: the places in the top 10% of that party’s vote, compared with every place in the study.

The bars are the same comparison as a percent. Blue, to the right means the trait is higher where the party does well. Red, to the left means it is lower. +100% means twice the average. −50% means half the average. A trait that is missing from the average entirely is listed as “not on average” and left off the chart.

Which parties rise and fall together

Each cell is a pair of parties. Red means they are strong in the same places. Blue means when one is strong, the other is weak. The number runs from −1 (always opposite) to +1 (always together). Zero means no link.

This is a rank correlation: it asks whether the places line up in the same order for both parties, so one huge party cannot dominate the picture.

Shares in one place add up to 100%. A gain for one party is a loss for the others, so the correlations look weaker than they would if each party were counted on its own. The useful question is whether parties inside a bloc stick together more than parties across the aisle.

Do the blocs stick together?

Each row is the average rank correlation between parties in that group. A number near 1 means those parties tend to be strong in the same places. A number near 0 means no correlation.

Who is the most different from its own bloc?

Take one party out and compare it with the rest of its bloc. Each partner counts the same, so Likud cannot define the coalition just by being large. A number near 1 means it wins where its partners win. A low number means it has its own map.

Who finishes next to whom

In each place, the parties that received any votes are lined up from first to last. Two parties are “next to each other” when one sits immediately above or below the other in that line.

Sitting next to each other is not the same as rising and falling together. The two biggest lists are often first and second, so they sit next to each other even when their shares move in opposite directions.

Neighborhoods the census does not explain

The census model guesses a party’s share from who lives in the neighborhood. These are the places where the real vote is far from that guess. Red means the real share is higher than the census guess: the census guessed too low. Blue means the real share is lower than the census guess: the census guessed too high.

Only neighborhoods with more than 300 valid ballots and a gap bigger than 10 percentage points are kept. The table lists the five largest gaps in each direction. Some parties have fewer than five.

Does support sit in clusters?

A tall bar means the party’s strong places sit next to other strong places, and weak next to weak. Support is geographic. A short bar would mean the map looks scattered. Every party in this election clusters. The differences are in how tight the clusters are.

Red is strong support next to strong support. Blue is weak next to weak. Orange is a strong pocket surrounded by weaker support. Purple is a weak pocket surrounded by stronger support. All four layers start on. Use the layer list to hide any of them.

How much place explains

Each bar is how much of the difference between places that description can explain. 100% would be a perfect explanation. 0% would mean it learns nothing. Who lives there explains more than the shops, schools, and streets within a seven-minute walk.

Where the numbers come from

  • Votes. Central Elections Committee, Knesset 26 (2026), by ballot box and by locality.
  • Polling stations. GovMap points for Knesset 26, so each station has a location.
  • Neighborhoods. Neighborhood boundaries and neighborhood vote counts, used for the winner map and the places that do not fit.
  • Who lives there. Central Bureau of Statistics, Census 2022.
  • The street outside. Shops, schools, parks, crime, shelters, and transit within a seven-minute walk.

How this was built

Ballot boxes at the same address are added into one polling place. Each place takes the nearest polling station within 1 km. Places with no station that close are left out. Polling places with fewer than 150 voters are dropped. A party’s share is its votes divided by valid ballots there.

Neighboring places often share one polling station, so they carry the same result. Clustering looks stronger here than it would on a map of separate polling stations.

The census, and what is within a seven-minute walk, are attached to the same place. The portraits use the places where that party’s share is in its own top 10%. For the gaps, those places are gathered into a neighborhood and the guess is compared with the real neighborhood vote.

What this cannot say

  • These are places, not people. A trait of a neighborhood is not a trait of every voter who lives there.
  • Because shares add up to 100%, parties look less linked than they would on their own.
  • Shared polling stations make neighboring places look more alike than separate stations would.
  • The census is from 2022. The vote is from 2026.
Places, not people. Party names are written in full throughout.
