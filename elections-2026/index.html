Which parties rise and fall together

Each cell is a pair of parties. Red means they are strong in the same places. Blue means when one is strong, the other is weak. The number runs from −1 (always opposite) to +1 (always together). Zero means no link.

This is a rank correlation: it asks whether the places line up in the same order for both parties, so one huge party cannot dominate the picture.

Do the blocs stick together?

Each row is the average rank correlation between parties in that group. A number near 1 means those parties tend to be strong in the same places. A number near 0 means no correlation.

Who finishes next to whom

This shows whose voters live closer to each other. Neighbors are taken from the top 4. In each place, the parties that received any votes are lined up from first to last, and only those four count. Two of them are “next to each other” when one sits immediately above or below the other. A party in fifth place or lower is not a neighbor.

Sitting next to each other is not the same as rising and falling together. The two biggest lists are often first and second, so they sit next to each other even when their shares move in opposite directions.

The percentage uses every place where that party got any votes, including places where it finished fifth or lower.

Assumption: if voters in one place are homogeneous, then vote neighbors show which parties appeal to similar people, and those parties probably belong in one bloc.

How this was built

Everything on this site is measured on one grid of hexagons (H3 resolution 10, about 150 meters across). Votes, the census, and the surroundings are each moved onto that grid, so they can be compared hexagon by hexagon. On this site a “place” is one of these hexagons.

Votes on the hexagon

Ballot boxes at the same address are added into one polling place. Each hexagon takes the polling place nearest to its center, measured as a straight line. A hexagon with no polling place within 1 km is left out. A polling place where fewer than 150 people voted is dropped, and every hexagon assigned to it goes with it. A party’s share is its votes divided by the valid ballots at that polling place.

One polling place usually serves many hexagons, and all of them carry the same result. So that a large polling place does not count many times over, each hexagon is weighted by the people who voted there divided by the number of hexagons sharing it. A polling place then counts once, in proportion to its voters. Every average, median, and model on this site uses that weight.

Census on the hexagon

The census is published by statistical area, not by hexagon. A statistical area is the smallest unit of the Central Bureau of Statistics, usually a few thousand residents. To move it onto the grid, each hexagon is laid over the statistical-area map. Each census value of a hexagon is the average of the statistical areas that cover it, weighted by how much of the hexagon each one covers. An area that covers 5% of the hexagon or less is ignored. Household size is a plain average of the covering areas. The main religion is taken from one of them.

A statistical area is much larger than a hexagon, so most hexagons inside one area get the same census values. The census side of this site is only as detailed as the statistical areas.

All census data aggregated on the hexagon comes from the Mapinamo platform.

Surroundings on the hexagon

The surroundings are measured inside a 7-minute walk from each hexagon: crime level, daily shops, clinics and hospitals, cafes and restaurants, nightlife, fashion, culture, beauty, schools, kindergartens, synagogues, colleges, parks, parking, malls, offices, public transport accessibility (bus routes, train and light rail stations), bomb shelters, and how varied the places are. Missile alerts are counted inside the hexagon itself.

These figures come from the Mapinamo platform, created from Overture Maps, OpenStreetMap, Israel Police crime reports, and GTFS files.

How the portraits are made

Each portrait answers one question: which traits set apart the places where a party is strong? It is built in three steps.

1. A model learns each party’s map. For each party, a machine-learning model (a random forest) learns to predict that party’s share on each hexagon. There are two models per party. The census model uses about 100 census traits, such as age bands, country of birth, year of immigration, schooling, industry, commute, wage, household religiosity, and main religion. The surroundings model uses the 22 measures of the surroundings described above. Each model is trained on a random 80% of hexagons and tested on the other 20%. Under each portrait, the test score is shown as the share of the place-to-place differences the model accounts for.

2. The model ranks the traits. For every hexagon, the model can say how much each trait raised or lowered the predicted share. Averaged over many hexagons, this gives each trait a rank by how much it matters, and a direction: whether a high value of the trait goes with a higher or a lower share.

3. Strong places are compared with all places. A party is strong on a hexagon when its share is in that party’s own top 10% of hexagons. For each trait, the typical level where the party is strong is compared with the typical level across all hexagons. A trait enters the portrait only when three things hold:

  • The model gives it a clear direction.
  • The gap between strong places and all places points the same way as the model.
  • The gap is large enough to describe a place: at least 3 percentage points for a percent, or otherwise a fifth of the usual spread of that trait.

The sentence uses the five highest-ranked traits that pass. The bars show up to twelve. The numbers in the sentence are the typical levels from step 3, so they are real values from the data, not model predictions.

What this cannot say

  • These are places, not people. A trait of a neighborhood is not a trait of every voter who lives there.
  • A portrait shows which traits go with a party’s vote, not what causes it.
  • The votes at a hexagon are the votes of the nearest polling place, not of that hexagon. Neighboring hexagons often share one polling place, so clustering looks stronger here than it would on a map of separate polling places.
  • Census values come from statistical areas, which are much larger than a hexagon.
  • The census is from 2022. The vote is from 2026.
